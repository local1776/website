What happened?
On March 27, 2025, the president signed an order declaring that more than 40 agencies and subdivisions have a primary function of national security work. That one finding removed them from the federal bargaining law. A second order in August added more. Contracts were cancelled, grievances were dropped, and dues deductions were shut off.
Was that legal?
The courts are split. Trial judges in Washington, San Francisco and Rhode Island found the orders likely unlawful or retaliatory. Two appeals courts let the orders take effect anyway, deferring to the president on national security. A third appeals court went the other way in May 2026.
Who was spared?
Police, firefighters and security guards. Customs and Border Protection, home of the Border Patrol union that endorsed the president. And at Veterans Affairs, smaller unions the department said had caused it little trouble.
Doesn't the bill that passed the House fix this?
It fixes 2025. The Protect America's Workforce Act cancels the two orders and restores the contracts. It does not touch the section of law that made the orders possible.
What does this proposal do?
It rewrites Chapter 71 from end to end. The national security exception becomes narrow, justified in writing, approved by Congress, and reviewable by a court that does not have to take the president's word for it. The rest of the chapter gets what it has never had: real penalties for breaking it, referees a president cannot bench, and contracts that hold.
Why not wait for a friendly president to sign a new order?
Because the next unfriendly one will cancel it. That has been the pattern since 1962. Rights that depend on who won the last election are not secure, and federal workers have spent fifty years defending the same ground. Only Congress can change the statute, and the statute is what lasts.
The bottom line
A right that one person can erase with a signature is not a right. Congress created federal bargaining by law, and Congress should be the one to decide who is left out of it.

The fix

Rewrite Chapter 71 so the rights in it can be enforced

Two tracks. One stops the damage now. The other makes sure it cannot be repeated.

Executive orders are written in pencil

Federal bargaining began as an executive order in 1962, and for 16 years it lasted only because each president chose to keep it. Every gain made by order since then has been erased by the next president who wanted it gone. Clinton's partnership order was revoked by his successor. The 2018 orders were cancelled in 2021 and came back in broader form in 2025.

A friendly president can sign a good order in an afternoon. An unfriendly one can cancel it just as fast. Only a statute outlasts an election.

Strike while the iron is hot

The Congress seated on January 3, 2027 will have the largest House majority since 2008 and at least 54 Democratic seats in the Senate. That is short of the 60 votes needed to end a Senate filibuster and the two-thirds needed to override a veto, so nothing is automatic.

It is enough to pass a bill through the House, put every senator on the record, attach a funding bar to bills the president has to sign, and have the full rewrite written and ready for the day it can become law. Majorities like this do not last. The time to write the statute is now.

Track one · now

Cut off the money

Attach one sentence to the next spending bill or defense bill: no funds may be used to carry out Executive Orders 14251 or 14343. The House already passed language like this once, in its version of the fiscal 2026 defense bill.

Track two · permanent

Rewrite Chapter 71

A fix for each of seven issues across the whole statute: who is covered, what can be bargained, who referees, how the law is enforced, and what a union needs to function. Introduce it now and build the record.

Issue 1
Who is coveredNarrows the national security exception and takes it out of one person's hands: written findings, approval by Congress, court review, a sunset, no delegation. Makes the same change for the Foreign Service. Brings TSA officers under the chapter and narrows the exclusions for VA medical staff. Tightens the definitions that keep employees out of bargaining units. Bars any future personnel system from waiving the chapter unless Congress says so in express terms.
Issue 2
What can be bargainedMakes staffing, technology and methods of work mandatory subjects instead of optional ones. Writes the long-standing bargaining threshold and the duty to bargain during a contract into the statute. Stops government-wide rules and executive orders from taking subjects off the table or voiding agreements. Limits an agency head's power to veto a signed contract.
Issue 3
Referees a president cannot benchA career deputy acts automatically when the General Counsel's seat is empty, and regional directors can issue complaints. Every stage of a case gets a deadline. Bargaining impasses go to neutral arbitrators chosen by the parties. When the board cannot or will not act, unions can go to court.
Issue 4
Enforcement with teethA right to sue in federal district court. Injunctions available to unions, and required when an agency repudiates a contract. Civil penalties for willful violations. Full make-whole relief, including lost dues, interest and attorney fees. Discipline for officials who order violations. Board orders that take effect without a second lawsuit. No unilateral changes while a dispute is pending.
Issue 5
Contracts and arbitration that holdUnions can get a court order compelling an agency to arbitrate. Arbitrators' awards get deference, with review in court. The list of subjects excluded from grievances is fixed in the statute. Agencies comply while an appeal is pending.
Issue 6
The union's ability to functionProtects official time, dues withholding, and reasonable access to the workplace, to employees and to information from limits imposed by order or guidance.
Issue 7
Restoration and purposeVoids Executive Orders 14251 and 14343 and restores every agreement in effect on March 26, 2025. Older exclusion orders expire in two years unless reissued under the new rules. Adds a rule that the chapter is to be read broadly to protect the right to bargain.

Current law · 5 U.S.C. § 7103(b)

(b)(1) The President may issue an order excluding any agency or subdivision thereof from coverage under this chapter if the President determines that—

(A) the agency or subdivision has as a primary function intelligence, counterintelligence, investigative, or national security work, and

(B) the provisions of this chapter cannot be applied to that agency or subdivision in a manner consistent with national security requirements and considerations.

(2) The President may issue an order suspending any provision of this chapter with respect to any agency, installation, or activity located outside the 50 States and the District of Columbia, if the President determines that the suspension is necessary in the interest of national security.

That is the whole provision. No definitions, no findings, no notice, no review, no time limit.

Proposed · discussion draft

(b)(1) Subject to section 7136, the President may issue an order excluding any subdivision of an agency from coverage under this chapter if the President determines, in writing, that—

(A) the subdivision has as its primary function intelligence, counterintelligence, or national security work;

(B) a majority of the employees of the subdivision are primarily engaged in such work; and

(C) the provisions of this chapter cannot be applied to that subdivision in a manner consistent with national security requirements and considerations, taking into account the authority reserved to agency management under section 7106 and the exclusion of employees from appropriate units under section 7112(b)(6).

(2) Subject to section 7136, the President may issue an order suspending any provision of this chapter with respect to any installation or activity of an agency located outside the 50 States and the District of Columbia, if the President determines, in writing, that the suspension is necessary in the interest of national security.

(3) An order under this subsection may not exclude from coverage under this chapter, or suspend any provision of this chapter with respect to, the whole of an Executive department, a military department, or an independent establishment.

(4) The authority of the President under this subsection may not be delegated.

(5) For purposes of this subsection and section 7136—

(A) ‘national security work’ means work that directly concerns the protection of the United States from foreign aggression, espionage, sabotage, international terrorism, or subversion, and does not include work the relationship of which to national security is economic, regulatory, scientific, or related to public health; and

(B) ‘subdivision’ means an organizational component of an agency below the level of the agency.

New section 7136, in summary (full text on the next tab)

The draft adds a new section to the chapter that governs every order under subsection (b). The order and its written findings go to Congress and to each affected union on the day it is signed. It takes effect only when Congress enacts a joint resolution approving it, and lapses if Congress does not act within 180 days. Where employees are already in a recognized bargaining unit, the president has to show what changed since the unit was approved and why management's existing powers are not enough. It may not rest on a union's exercise of its rights, its lawsuits or its politics. Recognition, contracts and dues withholding continue until the order takes effect and any court challenge is decided. A federal district court reviews the order fresh, with the burden on the government. An approved order expires after four years.

Discussion draft prepared by LIUNA Local 1776. It has not been reviewed by legislative counsel. The draft also amends the Foreign Service law, voids the 2025 orders and restores the contracts they cancelled. Management keeps its existing power to act in an emergency under section 7106(a)(2)(D), and individual employees doing security work can still be kept out of bargaining units under section 7112(b)(6).

Proposed new section · discussion draft

§ 7136. Orders under section 7103(b)

(a) Definitions.—In this section—

(1) the term ‘covered order’ means an order issued under paragraph (1) or (2) of section 7103(b); and

(2) the term ‘affected labor organization’ means, with respect to a covered order, a labor organization that is the exclusive representative of employees in a subdivision, installation, or activity to which the order applies.

(b) Transmittal and publication.—On the date on which the President issues a covered order, the President shall transmit to each House of Congress and to each affected labor organization, and shall publish in the Federal Register—

(1) the covered order;

(2) the written determination required under section 7103(b), including, for each subdivision, installation, or activity to which the order applies, a statement of the facts and reasons on which the determination is based; and

(3) the number of employees, and each appropriate unit, to which the order applies.

Classified information that supports a determination may be transmitted to Congress in a classified annex.

(c) Units with exclusive recognition.—

(1) Additional findings.—In the case of a covered order that applies to employees in an appropriate unit for which a labor organization has been accorded exclusive recognition under section 7111, the written determination required under section 7103(b) shall include, with respect to each such unit—

(A) a statement of the specific facts demonstrating that the application of this chapter to the unit during the period of exclusive recognition has impaired, or is likely to impair, national security;

(B) an explanation of why the authority reserved to agency management under section 7106, including the authority under section 7106(a)(2)(D) to take whatever actions may be necessary to carry out the agency mission during emergencies, is not sufficient to address that impairment;

(C) an explanation of why the exclusion of individual employees from the unit under section 7112(b)(6) is not sufficient to address that impairment; and

(D) a description of any material change in the functions of the employees in the unit since the date on which the unit was determined to be appropriate under section 7112.

(2) Effect of prior determination.—In any civil action under subsection (g), a determination by the Authority under section 7112 that a unit is appropriate, including any determination that employees in the unit are not described in section 7112(b)(6), shall be presumed to be correct, and that presumption may be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence of a material change in the functions of the employees in the unit since the date of the determination.

(d) Prohibited bases.—A covered order may not be based, in whole or in part, on—

(1) the exercise by any employee or labor organization of any right under this chapter;

(2) the filing of any grievance, charge, complaint, or civil action by any employee or labor organization;

(3) the speech, association, or political activity of any employee or labor organization; or

(4) any distinction among labor organizations, or among employees performing substantially similar functions, other than a distinction based on the criteria set forth in section 7103(b)(1).

(e) Congressional approval.—

(1) In general.—A covered order shall take effect on the date of the enactment of a joint resolution of approval with respect to the order.

(2) Failure to enact.—If a joint resolution of approval with respect to a covered order is not enacted before the end of the 180-day period beginning on the date of transmittal under subsection (b), the order shall have no force or effect.

(3) Joint resolution of approval defined.—In this subsection, the term ‘joint resolution of approval’ means a joint resolution the matter after the resolving clause of which is as follows: ‘That Congress approves the order issued by the President under section 7103(b) of title 5, United States Code, and transmitted to Congress on ______.’, the blank space being filled in with the appropriate date.

(4) Expedited procedures.—[Committee discharge, privileged motion to proceed, limits on debate and amendment, and the rulemaking clause, to be supplied by legislative counsel on the model of section 802 of this title.]

(f) Continuation of recognition and agreements.—Until the date on which a covered order takes effect under subsection (e), and thereafter until the entry of final judgment in any civil action commenced under subsection (g) not later than 60 days after that date—

(1) each exclusive recognition, each collective bargaining agreement, and each allotment under section 7115 that applies to employees covered by the order shall remain in full force and effect; and

(2) no agency may terminate its participation in any proceeding under section 7118, 7119, 7121, or 7122 by reason of the order.

(g) Judicial review.—

(1) In general.—An affected labor organization, or an employee aggrieved by a covered order, may bring a civil action for declaratory and injunctive relief in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia or in the district court of the United States for the judicial district in which the employee is employed.

(2) Standard of review.—In a civil action under this subsection, the court shall determine de novo, without according any presumption of regularity to the covered order or to the determination of the President, whether the order complies with section 7103(b) and this section. The United States shall bear the burden of establishing such compliance by clear and convincing evidence.

(3) Classified information.—The court may examine in camera any classified information on which a determination is based.

(4) Jurisdiction.—The district courts of the United States shall have jurisdiction of a civil action under this subsection without regard to section 7123 and without regard to whether any party has sought, or could seek, relief from the Authority.

(5) Attorney fees.—The court may award reasonable attorney fees and costs to a prevailing plaintiff.

(h) Duration.—A covered order shall cease to have effect on the date that is 4 years after the date on which it takes effect under subsection (e), unless a subsequent covered order applying to the same subdivision, installation, or activity has taken effect under that subsection.

The facts

Three facts about federal unions that the talking points leave out

Membership is voluntary. Pay and benefits are set by Congress, not at the bargaining table. And the government's own numbers show the system is cheap to run. Each one comes from the statute or from a government report, linked under it.

Fact 01

Nobody has to join, and nobody has to pay

The federal government has no closed shop, no agency fee and no “fair share” payment. It never has. An employee can spend a whole career in a union-represented job without joining or paying a cent, and the union still has to represent that employee.

What the law says

Every employee has the right to join a union “or to refrain from any such activity, freely and without fear of penalty or reprisal.” 5 U.S.C. 7102

The union must represent everyone in the unit “without discrimination and without regard to labor organization membership.” 5 U.S.C. 7114(a)(1)

Dues come out of a paycheck only when the employee signs a written authorization. 5 U.S.C. 7115

That is why official time exists. A union that must represent non-members for free, and may not charge them, was given paid time to do it. AFGE, the largest federal union, says more than half of the employees it represents pay no dues.

Fact 02

Federal unions do not bargain pay or benefits

Congress sets federal pay, health insurance, retirement and leave by statute. The bargaining law takes anything “specifically provided for by Federal statute” off the table. What is left is working conditions: schedules, safety, fair procedures and how a change gets carried out.

What the law says

“Conditions of employment” do not include matters set by federal statute. 5 U.S.C. 7103(a)(14)(C)

Mission, budget, staffing levels, hiring, assignments and discipline are reserved to management. 5 U.S.C. 7106

Striking is an unfair labor practice and grounds for removal. 5 U.S.C. 7116(b)(7) and 7311

The Congressional Research Service puts it in one line: “Most federal employees cannot bargain over wages or benefits.” The other side agrees. A Heritage Foundation analyst wrote in 2025 that federal unions are, with few exceptions, “prohibited from bargaining for pay and most benefits.”

The exceptions are small and Congress wrote each one. About seven agencies bargain pay under their own statutes, and the Postal Service bargains pay under a separate law.

Fact 03

Bargaining is cheap, and where the savings were measured it paid for itself

The main taxpayer cost of federal bargaining is official time: paid hours employees spend representing coworkers in grievances and negotiations. In fiscal 2019 that came to 2.6 million hours and about $135 million across the government, or less than two hours a year for each represented employee. It is about one-tenth of one percent of federal pay and benefits.

What the record shows

An independent Booz Allen analysis of the Customs Service partnership with its union found about $1.25 back for every dollar spent, and $3 million in net savings from 1993 to 1998. Senate Hearing 112-417

The labor board reported that cases settled without litigation rose 47 percent under partnership. Kelly Air Force Base alone avoided $2 million in litigation costs, and NASA projected $40 million in savings from changes its labor-management groups proposed. Government Executive, 1996

A peer-reviewed study of federal partnerships found they reduced labor-management disputes. Industrial and Labor Relations Review, 2006

Conflict is the expensive part. GAO estimated that processing 6,448 unfair labor practice charges in one year cost about $25.9 million. GAO, 1982

The clearest current example is in the National Guard. The Pentagon's own estimate says replacing union-eligible technicians with military personnel will cost about $248 million more over five years. See the National Guard page.

What we could not findNo CBO, CRS or RAND study puts a single dollar figure on the net savings from federal bargaining, and we will not invent one. The studies that find public unions raise costs are studies of state and local governments, where unions bargain pay and benefits. One of the best known found the effect was largest for benefits. Federal unions bargain neither. Anzia and Moe

Myths and the record

What critics claim, and what the record shows

The arguments against federal bargaining come mostly from the Heritage Foundation, its Project 2025 and allied groups. Here are the main ones, next to the facts.

Where critics have a point, and our answer

  • Presidents of both parties have used this exception.True. Carter used it first, in 1979. The difference is scale and selectivity. For 45 years the list was intelligence offices and a few law enforcement units. In 2025 it became most of the government, minus the unions that endorsed the president or stayed out of his way.
  • Two appeals courts sided with the administration.True, at the preliminary stage. A third appeals court and trial judges in three districts have gone the other way. When courts split on what a law allows, the answer is for Congress to say what it meant.
  • The government undercounts what official time costs.GAO found the estimates ran 9 to 15 percent low at the agencies it sampled, and many offices keep poor records. We support better reporting. Even at the higher number, the cost is a rounding error in the federal payroll.
  • Bargaining can slow management down.Sometimes it does, and Congress can set firm timelines for it. Slow is not the same as dangerous, and the law already lets managers act first in an emergency.

    The AGR conversion

    What the plan does

    On May 11, 2026, the Defense Department sent Congress a proposal to phase out dual-status technicians over ten years. The House wrote it into its defense bill as section 1105. The Senate committee's bill has a similar section 513.

    • No new technicians may be hired starting October 1, 2028.
    • Technician positions convert to AGR or to Title 5 civilian positions. The stated end state is 89 percent AGR and 11 percent civilian.
    • An occupied position converts only with the employee's written consent.
    • Section 709 of Title 32 is repealed on October 1, 2038. After that date no technician can be paid.

    The Department's stated reasons are readiness and workforce management. Its proposal says dual status is a burden on the technician, that career paths are rigid and that running two personnel systems is complicated. A National Guard Bureau paper adds that hiring a technician takes about 90 days against 30 for an AGR. The adjutants general, the National Guard Association and the enlisted association support it.

    How we got there

    In June 2026 Local 1776 gave Congress a 44-page cost analysis with the spreadsheet behind it. The model is simple on purpose so anyone can check it.

    • The baseline is the law. The fiscal 2026 defense act authorizes 31,699 technicians and 56,827 AGRs in the Army and Air Guard.
    • The conversion is the Pentagon's. At 89 percent, about 28,212 technician positions become AGR and 3,487 become Title 5.
    • The premium is modest. $10,000 more per AGR position per year in the conservative scenario, $16,000 when allowances, health care and retirement are loaded in. Title 5 conversions are counted as costing nothing extra.
    • What is left out makes the number low. No transition costs, no retraining, no lawsuits, no lost state contributions, no loss of experience.

    These are our benchmarks, built from published Defense Department, CBO and CNA findings. They are not an official score. We have not found one from the Congressional Budget Office or GAO, and Congress should not vote until it has both.

    It was also the answer in 2017

    The Institute for Defense Analyses studied the question for the Pentagon at Congress's direction. It found that replacing technicians with AGRs costs the same or more in every reserve component.

    For the Army National Guard, converting every technician to a mix of 80 percent AGR and 20 percent civilian added about $398 million a year in program cost and $762 million a year when retirement and other long-term costs were counted. For the Air National Guard the figures were $156 million and $490 million.

    What a complete cost comparison has to count

    Nearly fifty years of studies, and none found savings

    • 1978The Congressional Budget Office told Congress that the average Army Guard or Reserve member cost about one-fifth as much as an active-duty soldier, counting pay and operating costs.
    • 1982Congress ordered a test of replacing technicians with full-time military. It ran from February 1979 to June 1980. The Pentagon reported no discernible effect on unit readiness, and GAO found the Army had no evidence that converting more positions would improve it.
    • 2013A Defense Department report to Congress found that Guard and Reserve members who are not mobilized cost about 15 percent as much as active-duty members. A CNA study for Congress the same year found that, for General Schedule technicians, AGR retirement costs the Pentagon 34 percent more than technician retirement. It recommended keeping the technician program.
    • 2017The Institute for Defense Analyses, working for the Pentagon, found the same or higher cost in every component when AGRs replace technicians.
    • 2024The Congressional Budget Office again listed the opposite move as a way to save money: replacing 80,000 military personnel in support jobs with 64,000 civilians would save about $17 billion over ten years.
    • 2026The Pentagon's own table for this proposal shows a net cost increase of $248 million in the first five years.

    It ends bargaining for good

    An executive order can be cancelled by the next president. A converted position cannot bargain under any president.

    AGR members serve on “full-time National Guard duty.” Federal law makes it a crime to enroll them in a union or to bargain on their behalf. Chapter 71 separately excludes members of the uniformed services from the definition of employee.

    Bargaining is not all that goes. A technician can take a firing to the Merit Systems Protection Board and a discrimination complaint to the EEOC, with a union representative alongside. An AGR member has military channels, and the proposal offers nothing to replace what is lost.

    It fixes nothing

    The Department has not named a job an AGR can do that a technician cannot. The proposal shows the opposite. One of its own subsections has to amend 32 U.S.C. 328(b) so that AGR members are allowed to do the maintenance and repair work technicians already do.

    The work does not change. The equipment does not change. What changes is who does it, what it costs and whether the people doing it have any rights on the job.

    Why we say ending bargaining is one of the drivers

    The proposal never mentions unions. The Department says this is about readiness. Here is the record that leads us to a different conclusion.

    • The adjutants general asked Congress to ban technician unions in 1978.Fifty-three of them, through the National Guard Association, asked that technicians be removed from the bargaining program. Congress refused.
    • The Guard Bureau tried three times in two years to pull technicians out of the civilian discrimination complaint process.Local 1776's records show the attempts: in 2013 by internal direction, in the fiscal 2014 defense bill, and in a 2015 reorganization plan. Each would have moved technician complaints into military channels with no union representative. LIUNA and the Association of Civilian Technicians opposed each one, and in 2016 Congress wrote technicians' civilian appeal and discrimination complaint rights into section 709.
    • Ohio's adjutant general walked away from a 45-year bargaining relationship in 2016.The Ohio Guard declared the bargaining law did not apply to technicians and shut off dues. It lost at the labor board, at the Sixth Circuit and at the Supreme Court, 7 to 2.
    • The Pentagon's 2017 study treated technician bargaining as a problem.It warned that bargaining over military aspects of the program could hurt readiness, and it noted that AGRs cannot unionize.
    • The Secretary has said where he stands.Asked by lawmakers in 2026 why he cancelled nearly every union contract in the Department, he said it “should not be subject to collective bargaining.”
    • Money is not the reason.By the Department's own estimate the conversion costs more. If savings were the goal, the civilian share would not be 11 percent.

    The alternative the Pentagon passed over

    If the goal is a simpler, more modern workforce, there is a way to get it that saves money and keeps every capability: convert technicians to Title 5 civilian positions. Congress did it for part of the workforce in 2017. LIUNA supports a full conversion.

    A Title 5 employee draws no housing or subsistence allowance, pays a share of health insurance and earns a civilian retirement. CBO's studies of replacing military personnel with civilians point the same way. The Pentagon chose a workforce that is 89 percent military instead.

    What Congress should do

    • Strike section 1105 of H.R. 8800 and section 513 of S. 4784 from the final fiscal 2027 defense bill.
    • No phase-out without a CBO score and a GAO review. Both should compare the Pentagon's plan with the status quo and with a full Title 5 conversion, on the same assumptions.
    • Keep the rule against coercion. The fiscal 2026 defense act already bars a state from pressuring a technician into conversion or punishing one who declines.
    • Keep civilian protections in place. Bargaining, appeal and discrimination complaint rights should stay with the workforce through any transition.
    • Name technicians and Title 5 Guard employees in the rewrite of Chapter 71, so neither the national security exception nor a position conversion can be used to empty their bargaining units.

    Where it stands

    The House passed its defense bill on July 22, 2026, by 216 to 212, with the phaseout in it. The Senate never passed its version. The four Armed Services leaders are expected to negotiate a final text in the lame-duck session, with a vote in December.

    Local 1776 gave the Senate committee its response in May and its cost analysis in June. AFGE and IFPTE have asked the negotiators to drop the phaseout. The decision will be made by the outgoing Congress, before the new majorities are seated on January 3.

    The window opens January 3

    Democrats will hold more than 250 House seats, their largest majority since 2008, and at least 54 seats in the Senate. That is a strong hand. It is not a supermajority. Ending a Senate filibuster takes 60 votes, and overriding a veto takes 290 in the House and 67 in the Senate.

    So the bill still needs Republicans. Twenty of them voted yes in the House in 2025. In the Senate, Lisa Murkowski is a cosponsor. Susan Collins, the other Republican cosponsor, lost her seat in November.

    What to ask for

    Pass the rewrite of Chapter 71 through the House early in the new Congress. Force a vote in the Senate. Put the funding bar on every spending bill and on the defense bill, which the president has to sign. Keep the full statute written, introduced and ready.

    A new executive order from a friendly president would not solve this. The next president could cancel it the same day. Long-term protection can only come from changing the law.
