Investigations

Garrity and Kalkines Warnings

Garrity Warnings

A Garrity warning is given when an employee is asked to give information voluntarily about their own possible misconduct, and the answers might be used in a future criminal proceeding. The employee is told they have the right to remain silent if the answers may incriminate them, that anything they say may be used against them in a criminal or administrative proceeding, and that they cannot be disciplined for remaining silent. The right comes from the Supreme Court’s decision in Garrity v. New Jersey, 385 U.S. 493 (1967). It is the one time an employee can remain silent in an investigation without being disciplined for failing to cooperate.

An employee given a Garrity warning will be asked to sign and date it. The warning states, in part:

  1. You are being asked to provide information as part of an investigation being conducted into alleged misconduct and for improper performance of official duties.
  2. This is a voluntary interview. Accordingly, you do not have to answer questions. No disciplinary action will be taken against you solely for refusing to answer questions.
  3. Any statement you furnish may be used as evidence in any future criminal proceeding or agency disciplinary proceeding, or both.

Kalkines Warnings

When the government has chosen not to prosecute an employee criminally and pursues only administrative discipline, the agency may require the employee to answer questions. If the statement will be used only to decide whether discipline is appropriate, and there is no foreseeable criminal liability, a Kalkines warning applies.

In Kalkines v. United States, 473 F.2d 1391 (Ct. Cl. 1973), the U.S. Court of Claims held that an employee cannot be disciplined for remaining silent unless told that their answers, and anything learned from them, cannot be used against them in a criminal case. The Fifth Amendment protects federal employees from self-incrimination, but once a Kalkines warning removes the threat of prosecution, that right no longer applies. An employee who still refuses to answer after being clearly told to do so may be removed from federal service for failing to cooperate.

The Kalkines warning tells the employee that:

  1. They will be asked specific questions about the performance of their official duties.
  2. They must answer, and discipline up to and including removal may follow if they refuse or fail to answer fully and truthfully.
  3. Their answers, and any information or evidence that results from them, may be used in civil or administrative proceedings.
  4. Neither their answers nor anything learned from them can be used against them in a criminal proceeding, except that knowingly and willfully false statements may be prosecuted.
